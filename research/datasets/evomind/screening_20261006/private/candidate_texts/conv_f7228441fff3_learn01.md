**Opening — ~50s**

Good morning, everyone. We are Group 8, and we chose Unit 5. Why? Our group has five people, so we thought the universe was giving us a sign! Just kidding. The real reason: this topic — technology transfer for the poor — means a lot to us. We care about how new ideas can reach the people who need them most.

When you hear "technology transfer", what do you think of? Many people think of rich countries selling expensive technology to poor countries — big companies win, local people lose.

But today, I want to question that idea. The article says technology transfer, done well, can be one of the strongest tools against poverty. How can we make it help everyone — even the poorest?

**Part 1: What Is Technology Transfer? — ~1 min**

First, what does technology transfer really mean?

Many people have a wrong idea. They think the key to growth is simply spending more money on research — the "science push" model. If a country spends more on R&D, growth will follow.

But the article makes an important point. Research money is only part of the picture. What matters more is how science is used — put into products, working methods, and skills for daily life. That is technology transfer.

In the past, it had a bad name. Critics said big companies used it to get rich at the cost of poor countries: they sold products people could not afford and kept know-how secret to keep countries dependent.

But things are changing. As developing countries join the world economy, East Asia and Latin America show that good technology transfer — learning from abroad, and increasingly building their own — is key to growth and better lives.

**Part 2: A Shared Job — ~1 min 5s**

So who makes technology transfer happen? Everyone — it is a shared job.

Private companies provide the most common channels. Why? Because the market is the fastest engine for new ideas. It gives business people a reason to try and rewards inventors with patents.

But the market alone forgets the poorest. It naturally serves those who can pay. So the gap we should worry about is no longer only between rich and poor countries — it is now inside developing countries too.

This is where governments come in, with three jobs.
One: invest in infrastructure — physical and intellectual. Pay for university research and training, so countries can receive technology, understand it, and use it.
Two: make rules. New technology must be not only useful, but also safe and acceptable. Governments need bodies to judge this, using international standards or their own.
Three — the hardest — build forms of technology transfer that help the poor directly.

**Part 3: Reaching the Poor — The India Example — ~1 min 10s**

A clear example from the article: India.

Ashok Khosla of Development Alternatives says India's workforce will grow by 40 million in three years. Everyone sees IT as the engine of growth — and it is impressive. But IT will create only about one million of those 40 million jobs.

The other 39 million need work in old sectors like farming and building. These sectors need technologies that create jobs, not replace workers.

Why is this hard? Such technologies rarely make enough money to attract investors, because the money runs to IT. They do not fit the market model. Without them, India faces a growing gap between rich and poor — and the social problems that come with it.

The article asks for two kinds of creative thinking.
First, new ideas for making things: new ways to make bricks helped people start small businesses. Simple ideas that create jobs.
Second, social creativity: mixing modern science with the wisdom of local communities, filling gaps the market ignores, like small-scale farming, and finding money without patent fights.

**Conclusion — ~40s**

Let me finish.

Technology transfer is not about money or machines. It is about how knowledge moves through society — and who is left behind. Private companies push innovation, but governments must make sure it serves the whole country, not just the market. The biggest challenge is reaching the poor — people whose needs the market does not reward.

The article's main message: developing countries need new forms of social business for the poor. To grow, they need a new kind of technology transfer — creative, open to everyone, and built around people, not only profits.

Thank you.
