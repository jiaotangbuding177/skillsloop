**Speech Script: Technology Transfer for the Poor** （5-Minute English Presentation · EPEBE · Group 8）

**1. Opening — approx. 45 sec**

Good morning, everyone.

Before we dive in, a quick introduction: we are Group 8, and we chose the topic from Unit 5. You might be wondering — why Unit 5? Well, we have five people in our group, so we figured the universe was giving us a sign! (pause for laugh) Just kidding. The real reason is that this topic — technology transfer for the poor — resonates deeply with all of us. We genuinely care about how knowledge and innovation can reach those who need it most, and that's why we're here today.

Let me start with a question. When we hear the phrase "technology transfer", what comes to mind? For many people, it means rich countries selling expensive technology to poor countries — a one-way flow that benefits corporations more than communities.

But today, I want to challenge that view. The article we're discussing, titled "Technology Transfer for the Poor", argues that technology transfer, when done right, can be one of the most powerful tools for fighting poverty. The question is: how do we make it work for everyone — including the poorest?

**Part 1: What Is Technology Transfer? — approx. 1 min**

First, let's clarify what technology transfer actually means.

There's a common misconception that the key to development is simply spending more money on research — what's called the "science push" model. If a country spends a bigger share of its GDP on R&D, the thinking goes, development will follow.

But the article makes a crucial point: research spending is only part of the picture. What matters more is how scientific knowledge is actually applied — embedded into products, processes, and skills that people can use. That process is technology transfer.

Historically, technology transfer had a bad reputation. Critics saw it as a way for multinational corporations to get rich at the expense of poor countries — selling products people couldn't afford and withholding technical know-how to keep them dependent.

But that's changing. As developing countries integrate into the global economy, regions like East Asia and Latin America have shown that effective technology transfer — drawing on knowledge from abroad and, increasingly, building their own — is essential for economic growth and social prosperity.

**Part 2: Shared Responsibility — approx. 1 min 15 sec**

So who makes technology transfer happen? The answer is: everyone. It's a shared responsibility.

In practice, the private sector provides the most widely used channels. Why? Because the market is the most effective engine for rapid innovation — it gives entrepreneurs incentives and rewards inventors through patents. That's a reality we can't ignore.

But the article warns that the market alone leaves the poorest behind. Private-sector technology transfer naturally targets those who can pay. And so the gap we should worry about is no longer just between rich and poor countries — it's the gap within developing countries themselves.

This is where governments step in. They have three key jobs:

- First, invest in infrastructure — both physical and intellectual. That means funding university research and training so that countries have the capacity to not just receive technology, but to understand and use it.
- Second, regulate. Transferred technologies shouldn't only be useful — they should be socially acceptable and safe. Governments need institutions that can make those judgments, whether by adopting international standards or creating their own.
- And third — and this is the hardest part — actively develop forms of technology transfer that directly benefit the poor.

**Part 3: Reaching the Poor — The India Example — approx. 1 min 15 sec**

Let me give you a concrete example from the article: India.

Ashok Khosla, from the organization Development Alternatives, points out that India's workforce will grow by 40 million people over just three years. Everyone looks to the IT industry as the engine of growth — and it is impressive. But here's the catch: IT is expected to create only about one million of those 40 million jobs.

The remaining 39 million people will need work in traditional sectors like farming and construction. And those sectors desperately need technologies that create jobs rather than replace workers.

The problem? Such technologies are rarely profitable enough to attract the investment capital that flows into IT. They don't fit the market model. But without them, countries like India face a growing divide between rich and poor — and all the social tensions that come with it.

So what's the solution? The article calls for creative thinking — two kinds, actually.

- The first is technical creativity. Khosla gives the example of new brick-making techniques that have helped launch small-scale enterprises. Simple, practical innovations that create livelihoods.
- The second is social innovation — combining modern science with the practical wisdom of traditional communities. Filling gaps the private sector ignores, like niche agriculture. Finding ways to attract investment without getting tangled in patent fights.

These approaches don't replace conventional technology transfer. But they offer something the market alone cannot: a real chance at sustained poverty reduction.

**Conclusion — approx. 45 sec**

So let me wrap up.

Technology transfer is not just about money or machines. It's about how knowledge flows through society — and who gets left behind. The private sector drives innovation, but governments must ensure that innovation serves the national interest, not just the market. And the biggest challenge of all is reaching the poor — people whose needs the market simply doesn't reward.

The article's core message is this: developing countries need new forms of social entrepreneurship to meet the needs of the poor. And for that entrepreneurship to flourish, they need new kinds of social technology transfer — creative, inclusive, and built around people, not just profits.

Thank you.
